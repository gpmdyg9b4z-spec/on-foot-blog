Introduction

A Hillside That Used
to Be Underwater

Most visitors reach Labuan Bajo as a stepping stone to somewhere else — the harbour town on the western tip of Flores where boats leave for Komodo National Park and its dragons. Gua Batu Cermin, the Mirror Stone Cave, sits a short drive inland, in Wae Sambi village, and is usually squeezed in as a half-day filler before or after a boat trip. It rewards more attention than that.

The cave sits inside a limestone hill roughly 75 metres high, a few kilometres from the town centre. What makes it worth the detour is not scale — this is a modest system, walkable in well under an hour — but what is embedded in its walls: coral, shells and other marine fossils, sitting in rock that is now well above sea level. The short version of the site's story, repeated by every guide and every tourism blog, is that the cave was once on the seabed and was pushed up by earthquake activity. The fuller version is a little more interesting, and comes later in this post.

Stop 1 · Origins

The Priest Who
Found the Cave

Gua Batu Cermin · Documented 1951

Batu Cermin's discovery is credited to Theodor Verhoeven, a Dutch Catholic priest based at a seminary on Flores through the 1950s and 60s, who spent his free time locating and excavating archaeological sites across the island. He is the reason the cave has a written history at all: it was Verhoeven who first documented the marine fossils in its walls and connected them to the theory that this stretch of coastline had once been seabed.

Verhoeven's real significance to science lies elsewhere on Flores. In the 1950s he began excavating a limestone cave near Ruteng called Liang Bua, uncovering Neolithic burials and, separately, stone tools that he controversially argued were far older than anyone was prepared to believe. He was largely dismissed by paleoanthropologists in his lifetime.

Decades later, in 2003, an Indonesian-Australian team excavating at Liang Bua found the remains of a tiny, unknown human species — nicknamed "the hobbit" and formally named Homo floresiensis. Verhoeven had died in 1990 without knowing he'd been right. The site he first opened up to research is now one of the most significant in the story of human evolution.

Batu Cermin was, by comparison, a minor find — a curiosity noted on the way to the more consequential caves further east. But it is the one modern visitors can actually walk through.

Stop 2 · Geology

Two Kinds
of Stone

Guides here tend to open with a basic distinction that turns out to explain most of what you're looking at: the hill is made of two different rocks, laid down in different ways. Older volcanic rock forms a base, and coral limestone — the compressed remains of an ancient reef — sits on top of it, generally in thicker, more voluminous deposits than the volcanic layer beneath.

"Because of the erosion, the air, the water — the slow — we can see everything."

The two rocks erode at different rates, which is why the hillside doesn't wear away evenly: the softer limestone above wears faster than the harder volcanic base, leaving the kind of stepped, undercut profile typical of tropical karst country. It's the same basic mechanism — differential erosion of stacked rock layers — that shapes mesas and hoodoos elsewhere in the world, just executed here in limestone over volcanic rock instead of sandstone over shale.

Stop 3 · The Name

Why It's Called
the Mirror Stone

The cave's name — batu cermin, "mirror stone" in Indonesian — refers to a specific light effect rather than a general shine. Sunlight enters through a narrow gap in the cave roof and, hitting certain wall surfaces at the right angle, appears to bounce and illuminate sections of the chamber that light shouldn't otherwise reach — as if the rock itself were reflecting rather than simply catching the light. It's most pronounced around midday, when the sun sits closest to directly overhead.

Guides here generally ask visitors not to touch the formations, on the grounds that the stone is still forming and that skin contact interrupts the process — oils from a hand can stop mineral deposition at that spot, and are also said to strip out the salt content that gives some surfaces their sheen. Whether or not a single touch measurably halts millennia of accretion, it's a reasonable house rule: cave formations everywhere are slow-growing and easily marked, and "don't touch" protects them regardless of the precise mechanism.

Stop 4 · What the Walls Show

Fossils, and
a Geological Correction

The walls carry the cave's most striking evidence: embedded coral, shell fragments and other marine fossils, visible without any digging or specialist eye. It's genuinely persuasive material — rock that clearly formed underwater, now dry and well above the waterline.

"You know that Labuan Bajo actually was underwater? At some point, that was going to fall."

— Guide

The story told on site — that the cave itself was once submerged, then thrust up whole by an earthquake — is the version every visitor hears, and it isn't quite right. The more precise account, set out by cave-documentation specialists who've surveyed the site, is this: the limestone was indeed formed underwater, as compressed coral reef, and was then lifted above sea level by tectonic uplift over a very long geological timescale — this part of Flores sits in an active subduction zone, and the whole coastline has been rising for millions of years.

But the cave — the hollow space you actually walk through — did not exist yet at that point. It formed afterwards, once the limestone was already dry land, through the ordinary karst process of rainwater slowly dissolving passages through the rock. So: the stone was underwater; the cave, as a walkable space, was not. It's a distinction worth preserving even though it complicates a good story.

None of which makes the fossils less real, or less worth the visit — it just means the more accurate framing is "rock that was once underwater, later hollowed out," rather than "an underwater cave that popped up intact."

Stop 5 · Formations

Shapes
in the Dark

Like most show caves, Batu Cermin has a set of formations that guides point out by resemblance rather than by mineral name — a game that's more about giving visitors something memorable to photograph than any scientific claim. A rounded outcrop near the entrance is nicknamed the mushroom stone (the alternative reading offered on the walk was "umbrella," and the debate was not resolved). Deeper in, a pale, elongated formation catching direct light is known locally as the Madonna or "the lady" — visitors are told it resembles a robed, standing figure, a comparison several nationalities of tourists apparently arrive at independently.

Not every shape gets a tidy label. One formation midway through the cave prompted a genuine back-and-forth on this visit: a monkey's head, someone suggested, or perhaps a dragon, or — the guide's own read — a mongoose, on account of the texture of the stone reading like skin or fur. This kind of open disagreement is normal in show caves: pattern-matching in low light is subjective, and no cave management enforces an official taxonomy of shapes. Other frequently cited resemblances nearby: a fish head, and a pair of turtles said to be sleeping side by side.

"When you just look with your eye, you see one thing. But zoom in, and you see the differences — different colours, even inside it."

Stop 6 · Life in the Cave

Bats, Growth
& Slow Time

The cave is home to a resident bat colony, along with birds that nest in the darker recesses and cave-adapted insects. None of it is dramatic wildlife-spotting — the animals keep mostly to the ceiling and the unlit side-passages — but their presence is part of why guides ask visitors to keep voices down and torches steady rather than swept around freely.

75m
Height of the limestone hill containing the cave, first documented by Theodor Verhoeven in 1951. The walk from the settlement to the cave mouth covers roughly 300 metres of path.

Inside, stalactites and stalagmites are at various stages of a very slow process: some have met in the middle to form solid columns, others are still separate, still dripping, still years or millennia short of connecting. Guides tend to offer growth-rate figures — a few centimetres per many thousands of years was the estimate given on this walk — and while exact rates vary enormously by cave depending on water flow and mineral content, the general order of magnitude (thousands of years per centimetre, not years) holds across limestone caves worldwide.

Visiting Batu Cermin: Located in Wae Sambi village, roughly 10–20 minutes by car from central Labuan Bajo or Komodo Airport. A local guide is effectively mandatory and generally included in the entrance fee; helmets and torches are provided. Expect to duck and crouch through several low sections — closed shoes recommended, and it can be muddy after rain. Visit around midday for the best chance of seeing the mirror-light effect the cave is named for. Allow 30–60 minutes.
